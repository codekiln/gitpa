15 Using Scales
  • Microfreak/UG/15 Using Scales/01 Scale Settings

    • 15.1 Scale settings

      • Utility offers scale selection in two places:
        • Utility > Misc > Scale sets the scale globally for keyboard playing, sequences, and arpeggios.
        • Utility > Preset > Scale sets the scale for the current preset.

      • Note/Info Save the same preset several times with different Scale or Root note settings to keep multiple versions.

      • The Scale option filters chromatic notes into a scale. The manual lists these note sets:
        • Major: C, D, E, F, G, A, B
        • Minor: C, D, E♭, F, G, A, B♭
        • Harmonic Minor: C, D, E, F, G, A♭, B
        • Dorian: C, D, E♭, F, G, A, B♭
        • Mixolydian: C, D, E, F, G, A, B♭
        • Blues: C, D, E♭, F, G♭, G, A♭, A, B♭
        • Pentatonic: C, D, E, G, A
      • To hear scale quantization, switch on the MicroFreak, choose a simple sound such as the “keys 2” template preset 131, then open Utility > Preset > Scale and press the preset select knob to choose a scale.
      • With Major selected, the white keys play the major scale. Black keys are also quantized into that scale: the key that normally plays C♯ plays C, and each black key is lowered by a semitone to fit the scale. Chords played on the keyboard therefore use notes from the selected major scale.
      • To compare scale colors, press Arpeggio and hold C, the third step, and the fifth step to play a C major chord. In Utility > Preset > Scale, turn the encoder to hear how the third changes in Minor, Dorian, and Blues.

      • Note/Info Start an arpeggio or sequence in one scale, then cycle through Utility > Misc > Scale to hear it change scale while playing.

15 Using Scales
  • Microfreak/UG/15 Using Scales/02 The Scale Root

    • 15.2 The Scale Root

      • Starting a scale on a different note changes its mood because the intervals are heard in a different order. Starting C major on C follows tone, tone, semitone, tone, tone, tone, semitone. Keeping that interval pattern and making D the root gives D, E, F♯, G, A, B, C♯, D.
      • Changing the Root is an intelligent transpose: it preserves the scale's interval structure. A simple transpose shifts every step by a semitone or whole tone.
      • Utility offers Root selection in two places:
        • Utility > Misc > Root changes the root globally for keyboard playing, sequences, and arpeggios.
        • Utility > Preset > Root changes the root for the current preset.

      • Note/Info Search for “music theory” to learn more about scales.

16. Paraphonic Chord Mode
  • Microfreak/UG/16 Paraphonic Chord Mode/01 Unison

    • 16.1. Unison

      • The MicroFreak has four oscillators. In standard Paraphonic mode, play them as chords on the keyboard.
      • Hold Shift and press the Paraphonic button to enter Unison mode. The button blinks. Keep the Paraphonic button held, release Shift, and turn the Preset encoder to set oscillator detune (unison spread).
      • All four oscillators play together at slightly different pitches, creating a thicker sound.

      • Note/Info Press Hold to free both hands.

      • The display shows spread from 0.001 to 12.000, with 12.000 equal to an octave.
      • Press the Paraphonic button to turn off Unison mode.
16. Paraphonic Chord Mode
  • Microfreak/UG/16 Paraphonic Chord Mode/02 Unison Defaults

    • 16.2. Adjusting the default Unison settings

      • By default, Unison uses all four voices. In Utility > Preset > Unison Count, choose how many voices Unison uses. Assigning three voices leaves one voice available for duophonic keyboard playing.
      • Set the default Unison spread in Utility > Preset > Unison > Spread.
16. Paraphonic Chord Mode
  • Microfreak/UG/16 Paraphonic Chord Mode/03 Spread Modulation

    • 16.3. Unison Spread as a modulation target

      • Assign modulation to Unison spread in the Matrix by holding an Assign button and pressing the Paraphonic button.
      • To modulate oscillator spread with the Cycling Envelope:
        • Hold Shift and press Paraphonic to enter Unison mode.
        • Select the Cycling Env/Assign 2 Matrix crossing and press the Matrix encoder to set its modulation amount to 90.
        • Set the Cycling Envelope to Env mode, with Rise at 2.5 seconds, Fall at 150 milliseconds, and Hold/Sus at 0 milliseconds.
        • Turn the amount knob to explore the modulation intensity.
      • The Cycling Envelope changes Unison spread when you press a key or when the arpeggiator or sequencer triggers its next step.

      • Note/Info Modulating Unison spread can work especially well with Vocoder presets.

      • Unison is available with every oscillator model.

  • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/02 CV Gate

    • 17.1 CV/Gate functions

      • The MicroFreak puts USB, MIDI, Clock, and CV/Gate outputs on its rear panel. This section describes its CV/Gate circuitry.
      • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/02 CV Gate/01 Pitch Gate and Pressure

        • 17.1.1 Control voltages: Pitch, Gate and Pressure

          • When Sequence A or B is selected, or when you play the keyboard, notes are immediately converted to Control Voltage (CV) and Gate signals at the rear-panel outputs. In Paraphonic mode, notes played on the keyboard take priority over a running sequence.
          • CV Gate outputs
          • Each note sends three independent voltages: Pitch, Gate, and Pressure. Pressure can send velocity or pressure, depending on the Utility setting or MIDI Control Center.
          • Some analog synthesizers implement CV/Gate in ways that are not fully compatible with the MicroFreak. Check their specifications before buying to ensure they work together.
          • MIDI Control Center can configure the response of the CV/Gate jacks. By default, Pitch uses the 1 V per octave standard: an octave played on the keyboard raises the connected synthesizer or Eurorack module by one octave. Some synthesizers use Hz/V or 1.2 V per octave; choose the matching Pitch Format in Utility or MIDI Control Center.
          • CV Gate setting in the MIDI Control Center
          • Gate output can use S-Trig, V-Trig 5 V, or V-Trig 10 V, set in Utility or MIDI Control Center. Keyboard sensitivity adjusts the CV pressure range to match an external modular synthesizer.
          • The default 1 V per octave standard means that one volt raises an oscillator by one octave. It is the most common standard. If external oscillators do not track properly, check the external gear's documentation; changing the Volt/Octave setting may help.
          • The Pitch control voltage can use:
            • 1 V per octave (0–10 V)
            • Hertz per Volt, for systems where each volt change produces a fixed change in frequency rather than a fixed musical interval
            • 1.2 V per octave, a Buchla-specific standard
          • CV setting in the MIDI Control Center

    • 17.3 Controlling external and modular gear

      • Arturia's MicroBrute, MiniBrute, and MatrixBrute helped renew interest in analog synthesizers. Many musicians also use Eurorack modules to build individual instruments; Arturia's RackBrute provides a Eurorack case.
      • The MicroFreak follows Arturia's other products with interfaces for modular equipment. Its Pitch, Velocity, and Pressure outputs can control external Eurorack modules.
      • MIDI also controls external synthesizers and modular systems. Connect the MicroFreak to a MIDI-equipped synthesizer with a MIDI cable. To control Eurorack modules over MIDI, use a module that converts MIDI from the MicroFreak into Pitch and Gate signals.
      • MIDI can also control external synthesizer parameters such as filters and envelope generators, if the synthesizer has a MIDI input.

  • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/05 Local Control

    • 17.4 About Local Control

      • By default, the keyboard is connected to the sound engine (Local On), so pressing a key plays the MicroFreak. Turn Local Control off when controlling the MicroFreak externally over MIDI: the keyboard sends notes to MIDI out, but they do not reach the sound engine. The MicroFreak may then seem unresponsive.

      • Note/Info If the MicroFreak seems unresponsive, check whether Local Control was turned off.

      • Local Off is useful when an external sequencer controls the MicroFreak. It prevents double triggering when the sequencer sends keyboard notes back to the MicroFreak sound engine, a condition called MIDI echo.

  • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/06 MIDI Channels

    • 17.5 About MIDI channels

      • A central synthesizer or MIDI controller can control several instruments. MIDI has 16 channels so one device can send separate parts to other instruments on different channels.
      • The MicroFreak can transmit on all 16 channels at once or on a selected channel. By default, it transmits on all channels. To send a performance or sequence to a particular synthesizer or drum machine, set the MicroFreak's output channel to match; drum machines commonly use channel 10. Change the channel in Utility or MIDI Control Center.
      • MIDI input works the same way. To have a controller such as an Arturia BeatStep Pro control the MicroFreak on channel 2, set its input channel to 2 in Utility.
      • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/06 MIDI Channels/01 DAW Configuration

        • 17.5.1 MIDI and CV/Gate signals: DAW configuration

          • A DAW can play MIDI tracks on the MicroFreak when the track's MIDI channel matches the MicroFreak's input channel. The Utility settings also let you receive on one channel and send the keyboard's notes to the DAW on another. Set the input and output channels in Utility.

  • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/09 MIDI CC Control

    • 17.8 Using MIDI CC# codes for control

      • MicroFreak encoders send MIDI CC# data as you turn them. CC# codes control parameters on external synthesizers, modular systems, and software such as VCV Rack. They are parameter-control messages, separate from note messages.
      • CC# codes work like remote knobs. For example, the MicroFreak's Filter knob sends CC# 23; a value of 0 closes the filter and a value of 127 opens it fully. The MicroFreak has 20 CC# codes for its parameters.
      • CC# codes can also control plug-in parameters. Arturia's V Collection plug-ins have MIDI Learn, which can link a MicroFreak knob to a plug-in knob. For example, the MicroFreak's Filter knob can control filters in CZ V, DX7 V, and Buchla Easel V at the same time.
      • Consult the DAW and V Collection documentation for setup instructions.

      • Note/Info If CC# messages are not sent, check the knob-send-CC# setting in Utility or MIDI Control Center.

      • The tutorials below show how to use CC# codes to control VCV Rack modules.

  • Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/10 Tutorial MIDI CC

    • 17.9 Tutorial 3: sending CC# codes from the MicroFreak

      • Turning a MicroFreak knob sends a CC# code. If you know the code for a dial, slider, or switch, you can use it to control an external parameter.
      • This tutorial links the MicroFreak's Cyclic Envelope to the ADSR envelope in VCV Rack's demo patch. It assumes the patch from Microfreak/UG/17 Ext Gear/08 MIDI Tutorial VCV Rack is already set up to control VCO-1 and the ADSR.
      • In VCV Rack, click an empty area of the rack to open the module selector. Search for MIDI and add the MIDI-CC module.
      • In the MIDI-CC module, choose Arturia MicroFreak from the device list.
      • The module has 16 CC# controllers, numbered 0–15, that can link MicroFreak parameters to VCV Rack parameters. Sixteen patch points below the connection field correspond to its entries.
      • Select the first entry, 0; it changes to a dash. Turn the MicroFreak's Cyclic Envelope Rise knob. The first field displays 5, the Rise parameter's CC#.
      • Repeat for Fall, Hold, and Amount. Their CC# values appear in the connection fields.
      • VCV Rack MIDI-CC patch
      • Connect the patch points to the ADSR CV inputs:
        • First patch point to Attack
        • Second patch point to Decay
        • Third patch point to Sustain
        • Fourth patch point to Release
      • The Cyclic Envelope now controls the VCV Rack ADSR. Press the MicroFreak's ARP button, play a chord, and turn the Cyclic Envelope knobs to hear the result.
      • The VCV Rack MIDI-CC module can identify CC# codes. Select one of its 16 fields and turn a MicroFreak knob; the module displays the CC# if that knob sends one.
      • The connection works both ways: a VCV Rack output, sequencer, or modular system can control MicroFreak parameters. Use a module such as the Befaco VCMC to convert analog modular signals to MIDI CC# messages.

      • Note/Info Like note and velocity values, CC# values range from 0 to 127.

19. Appendix B: The MicroFreak Vocoder
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/01 Intro

    • 19.1. An Introduction to Vocoding

      • Bell Labs patented the first vocoder in 1939 to speed up telephone connections. Musical vocoders appeared about forty years later and became known for their robotic sound. Artists have since used them in many ways, from Kraftwerk's “Autobahn” to Imogen Heap's “Hide and Seek.”
      • A vocoder transfers qualities of one sound to another. Speech vowels have characteristic frequency peaks called formants; a vowel commonly has one to three prominent peaks, F1 through F3. The positions of these peaks vary with the speaker and the sound being made.
      • The modulator, usually your voice, supplies the changing formants. A bank of tuned filters analyzes them and applies them to a carrier, such as an oscillator wave. The carrier takes on the shape of your voice.
      • Speech also has a changing loudness contour. The vocoder tracks this contour along with the formants, so the carrier follows the way you articulate a word.
19. Appendix B: The MicroFreak Vocoder
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works

    • 19.2. How Does Vocoder Work?

      • The MicroFreak analyzes incoming sound with 16 tuned bandpass filters. As with the MicroFreak's own filter in BPF mode, each band emphasizes a limited range of frequencies.
      • An envelope follower tracks the loudness in each band. The resulting signals control matching filters on the carrier, allowing each formant peak to follow its own changing volume. The vocoder also recreates the overall loudness pattern at the end of the signal path.
      • MicroFreak Vocoder signal path
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/01 Resolution

        • 19.2.1. Resolution

          • Early vocoders used roughly 10 frequency bands, which could make speech sound indistinct. The MicroFreak uses 16 bands to recreate the voice more accurately.
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/02 Voice

        • 19.2.2. The Perfect Modulator: Your Voice

          • A useful vocoder sound depends on pairing the modulator with a suitable carrier. Speak or sing with clear, even exaggerated articulation to drive the analysis filters.
          • Plosives such as “p,” “t,” and “b,” and sibilants such as “s,” “ch,” and “z,” can confuse the filter banks. The vocoder's high-pass filter helps remove them. Its hiss function can add high-frequency energy to help the filters respond.
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc

        • 19.2.3. The Vocoder Oscillator

          • A carrier rich in overtones gives the vocoder more material to shape. Use the Wave knob to move through sawtooth, pulse width, and noise waves; see also Microfreak/UG/06 Dig Osc/03 Types/22 Vocoder.
          • The Digital Oscillator
          • Wave positions
            • 0%: sawtooth.
            • Around 11%: pulse wave at 50% duty cycle.
            • From 11% to 90%: increasing pulse width, reaching 97% duty cycle.
            • 91–100%: noise.

          • Note/Info The manual gives different duty cycles at Wave 90%: 97% here and 99% in Microfreak/UG/06 Dig Osc/03 Types/22 Vocoder.

          • Sawtooth, pulse-width, and noise waveforms
          • Timbre and Shape also control the Vocoder Oscillator.

          • Note/Info Oscillator Type cannot be modulated while the Vocoder Oscillator is active.

          • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc/01 Timbre

            • 19.2.3.1. Timbre Encoder

              • Timbre selects the frequency range used for analysis and resynthesis. Vowel formants peak at different frequencies: for example, a “u” may have peaks near 330 and 1260 Hz, with variation across voices.
              • Matching the range to the speaker, or to an instrument used as a modulator, helps the filters respond to the frequencies that matter. Monitoring fewer frequencies can improve response time and output.
              • Vocoder filter bands and frequency response
          • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc/02 Shape

            • 19.2.3.2. Shape Encoder

              • Shape sets the bandwidth of each bandpass filter. Higher settings narrow the filters' frequency response and produce more pronounced harmonics.

19. Appendix B: The MicroFreak Vocoder
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/07 Config

    • 19.7. Vocoder Configuration

      • The Vocoder settings can be adjusted as the voice or microphone changes. Global Utility settings affect every vocoder preset; settings under Utility > Preset are saved with the current preset.
      • The global settings can be changed on the MicroFreak or in the MIDI Control Center.

      • Mic Settings

        • Mic Gain: -12 dB to +59 dB, or Auto Gain (default).
        • Noise Gate: Off or a threshold; default -70 dB.
        • Mic Detection: Off or On (default).
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/07 Config/01 Preset Settings

        • 19.7.1. Preset related Vocoder settings

          • Two settings under Utility > Preset are saved with each preset:
            • Vocoder Hiss Mode
            • Vocoder Hiss Vol
          • Older vocoders added high-frequency noise (“hiss”) to the lower, voiced “buzz” range to make consonants easier to understand.

          • Vocoder Hiss Mode

            • Off: Only the lower, vowel-like buzz range is heard.
            • Switched: A detector distinguishes voiced sections from unvoiced sections. During an unvoiced section, white noise replaces the carrier oscillator. Output is gated by the modulator and follows its level.
            • Pass: The vocoded synth signal is mixed with the part of the microphone signal above 5 kHz, allowing the high frequencies of the voice through.

          • Vocoder Hiss Vol

            • Sets the level of hiss mixed with the microphone signal and the white-noise replacement level in Switched mode, from -20 dB to 0 dB.
19. Appendix B: The MicroFreak Vocoder
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/08 Global Settings

    • 19.8. Global Vocoder settings

      • Use Utility > Mic Settings to match the microphone input to your voice or an external modulator. The menu controls Mic Gain, Noise Gate, and Mic Detection.
      • Auto Gain is the default and suits average use with the Vocoder Edition microphone. A lavalier microphone or external audio source may need manual gain.
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/08 Global Settings/01 Mic Levels

        • 19.8.1. Setting microphone levels

          • When a vocoder preset is selected, the OLED VU animation shows the microphone input level: Low, Mid, or Skull. Aim for Mid; Skull indicates a level that can distort the vocoder output.
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/08 Global Settings/02 Gain

        • 19.8.2. What is gain?

          • Microphone signals are usually quiet, around -30 dB to -70 dB, and need amplification. The MicroFreak preamp can reduce or amplify the signal with a gain setting from -12 dB to +59 dB. Negative settings reduce the input; positive settings amplify it.
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/08 Global Settings/03 Set Gain

        • 19.8.3. How do you set gain?

19.2. How Does Vocoder Work?
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/01 Resolution

    • 19.2.1. Resolution

      • Early vocoders used roughly 10 frequency bands, which could make speech sound indistinct. The MicroFreak uses 16 bands to recreate the voice more accurately.
19.2. How Does Vocoder Work?
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/02 Voice

    • 19.2.2. The Perfect Modulator: Your Voice

      • A useful vocoder sound depends on pairing the modulator with a suitable carrier. Speak or sing with clear, even exaggerated articulation to drive the analysis filters.
      • Plosives such as “p,” “t,” and “b,” and sibilants such as “s,” “ch,” and “z,” can confuse the filter banks. The vocoder's high-pass filter helps remove them. Its hiss function can add high-frequency energy to help the filters respond.
19.2. How Does Vocoder Work?
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc

    • 19.2.3. The Vocoder Oscillator

      • A carrier rich in overtones gives the vocoder more material to shape. Use the Wave knob to move through sawtooth, pulse width, and noise waves; see also Microfreak/UG/06 Dig Osc/03 Types/22 Vocoder.
      • The Digital Oscillator
      • Wave positions
        • 0%: sawtooth.
        • Around 11%: pulse wave at 50% duty cycle.
        • From 11% to 90%: increasing pulse width, reaching 97% duty cycle.
        • 91–100%: noise.

      • Note/Info The manual gives different duty cycles at Wave 90%: 97% here and 99% in Microfreak/UG/06 Dig Osc/03 Types/22 Vocoder.

      • Sawtooth, pulse-width, and noise waveforms
      • Timbre and Shape also control the Vocoder Oscillator.

      • Note/Info Oscillator Type cannot be modulated while the Vocoder Oscillator is active.

      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc/01 Timbre

        • 19.2.3.1. Timbre Encoder

          • Timbre selects the frequency range used for analysis and resynthesis. Vowel formants peak at different frequencies: for example, a “u” may have peaks near 330 and 1260 Hz, with variation across voices.
          • Matching the range to the speaker, or to an instrument used as a modulator, helps the filters respond to the frequencies that matter. Monitoring fewer frequencies can improve response time and output.
          • Vocoder filter bands and frequency response
      • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/02 How It Works/03 Vocoder Osc/02 Shape

        • 19.2.3.2. Shape Encoder

          • Shape sets the bandwidth of each bandpass filter. Higher settings narrow the filters' frequency response and produce more pronounced harmonics.

19.7. Vocoder Configuration
  • Microfreak/UG/19 Appendix B Vocoder/07 Config/01 Preset Settings

    • 19.7.1. Preset related Vocoder settings

      • Two settings under Utility > Preset are saved with each preset:
        • Vocoder Hiss Mode
        • Vocoder Hiss Vol
      • Older vocoders added high-frequency noise (“hiss”) to the lower, voiced “buzz” range to make consonants easier to understand.

      • Vocoder Hiss Mode

        • Off: Only the lower, vowel-like buzz range is heard.
        • Switched: A detector distinguishes voiced sections from unvoiced sections. During an unvoiced section, white noise replaces the carrier oscillator. Output is gated by the modulator and follows its level.
        • Pass: The vocoded synth signal is mixed with the part of the microphone signal above 5 kHz, allowing the high frequencies of the voice through.

      • Vocoder Hiss Vol

        • Sets the level of hiss mixed with the microphone signal and the white-noise replacement level in Switched mode, from -20 dB to 0 dB.

      • Microphone signals are usually quiet, around -30 dB to -70 dB, and need amplification. The MicroFreak preamp can reduce or amplify the signal with a gain setting from -12 dB to +59 dB. Negative settings reduce the input; positive settings amplify it.

21. Appendix D: CC# Values
  • Microfreak/UG/21 Appendix D - CC Values/01 What are CC Values

    • 21.1 What are CC# values?

      • MIDI messages include note-on, gate, note-off, and velocity data. Note number and velocity values range from 0–127.
      • Control Change (CC) messages are separate from note-related MIDI messages. They let a MIDI-compatible device control parameters on another device.
      • Turning a MicroFreak knob transmits its CC message; sending that CC value to the MicroFreak changes the parameter as if the knob had been turned.
